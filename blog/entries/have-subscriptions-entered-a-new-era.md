I've spent the best part of twenty years working in subscription businesses, and I can't remember a time when the conversation around growth has shifted quite so much.

For years, the playbook was relatively simple. Acquire more customers, improve conversion, reduce churn and repeat. There were still millions of customers to go after, so most conversations naturally gravitated towards the top of the funnel. Marketing spend increased, acquisition channels multiplied and growth often came from finding the next customer.

That model worked well for a long time, but today, I'm not sure it's enough.

For more established businesses, acquisition has become harder and significantly more expensive. Even for newer subscription businesses, there comes a point where continually buying growth becomes an expensive habit rather than a sustainable strategy. At the same time, customers have become much more deliberate about where they spend their money. They compare products more easily, expect more from the services they pay for and don't think twice about cancelling something that no longer feels valuable.

The State of Subscriptions report from Recurly really brought this to life for me. Subscriber acquisition rates have fallen from 4.1% in 2021 to 2.8%, while free trial conversion has dropped from 46% to 33%. Interestingly, one in five new subscribers is actually a returning customer, suggesting that winning people back is becoming just as important as winning them in the first place. Recurly's conclusion was that retention is becoming the cornerstone of subscription growth.

I agree, but I think it goes a bit further. Retention isn't just a strategy; it's what happens when customers keep getting value.

Throughout my career I've often had the same thought when reviewing a subscription business. Companies invest millions building products, features, services and content, yet the average customer only ever experiences a fraction of what already exists. We naturally assume the next stage of growth comes from building something new, when quite often the opportunity is helping customers get more value from what they've already paid for.

That's one of the reasons I think AI has the potential to change subscription businesses in a much more meaningful way than simply reducing costs. Most of the discussion today focuses on automation and efficiency, but I think the bigger opportunity is using AI to help customers discover more relevant content, surface useful features, remove friction and make better decisions. If customers experience more value, they have more reasons to stay.

Trust also plays a much bigger role than many businesses appreciate. The latest Edelman Trust Barometer found that business remains the most trusted institution globally, but expectations continue to rise. Customers increasingly expect organisations to be competent, transparent and consistent. In a subscription business, trust isn't won when someone enters their card details. It's built every month they decide you're still worth paying for.

When I review a subscription business today, these are the first five conversations I'd want to have.

**1. How much value do customers actually experience?**

Not how much value exists in your proposition, but how much people genuinely discover and use. There is often a huge gap between the capability a business has built and the value customers actually realise.

**2. What happens after someone subscribes?**

Too many businesses treat onboarding as an implementation task rather than the beginning of the relationship. The first 90 days should help customers build habits, achieve success quickly and understand why they subscribed in the first place.

**3. What are you really measuring?**

Most businesses have dashboards full of activity metrics, but activity doesn't always equal value. I'd be looking for the behaviours that genuinely predict long-term retention and lifetime value, then building the organisation around improving those.

**4. How easy is it for customers to discover more?**

Whether it's products, content, features or services, I'd be asking how customers find the next piece of value. If discovery relies on luck, search or the customer already knowing what exists, there's probably a growth opportunity hiding in plain sight. 

**5. Is your organisation helping or hindering the customer experience?**

This brings me back to the idea of Seam Tax. Marketing, Product, Finance, Operations and Customer Service all influence whether customers succeed, but they're often measured independently. Customers don't experience departments. They experience how well those departments work together, and every unnecessary handover creates another opportunity to lose value.

The more I've thought about subscriptions over the last few months, the more I've come to believe that we're moving into a different era of growth. Acquisition will always matter, but I don't think it will be the thing that separates the winners.

The businesses that stand out over the next few years will be the ones that continually help customers discover more value, earn their trust over time and make staying feel like the obvious decision.

To me, that's a far more interesting growth conversation.
