I have been constantly ping-ponging the relationship between internal structure, culture, and business growth.



We have all been in those business review meetings where the objectives are being reviewed – everyone sits there nodding that they are on track, a gentle pat on the back for each department and team, yet the business’s core and most important metrics are not being hit. I often felt like the imposter in the room, saying ‘Great. Well done all, but we are not on track to hit our growth targets’.



Equally, when you look at a customer journey, different teams and departments are in charge of the ‘parts’ of the journey (acquisition – marketing, onboarding – product, pricing – finance…) with no accountability for the ‘sum’. 



It’s like a long ribbon (the customer), chopped into small sections (the org), stitched together with frayed seams, with customers being lost at each stitch. I am calling this the ‘seam tax’, and it should be a metric that is understood and closely monitored.



Business growth requires momentum, alignment, and a thread that represents the customer. It is easy to get lost in internal thinking and forget that customers don't experience departments; they experience your business.



I understand that old systems and cultures are hard to change, so the cracks are covered up and often forgotten. This is surely one of the biggest growth leaks? Growth isn’t just about product, marketing, and finance in solo. Customers have to align these teams more than ever before.



The NHS is a useful way to visualise this. A patient doesn't care where one team ends and another begins, they expect a connected service. Yet from ambulance to A&E to triage to diagnostics to treatment, every handover creates another seam. Every team has its own objectives, pressures and priorities, but the patient experiences the whole. That's what I mean by seam tax. It's the hidden cost of organisational boundaries getting in the way of the journey.



So, if you're sitting down to build your three-year strategy and targeting 10% year-on-year growth, I'd spend less time asking whether each function will hit its objectives or reviewing headcount on each team, and more time understanding where your ‘seam tax’ exists.



I have a feeling that's where a lot of untapped growth is hiding.
